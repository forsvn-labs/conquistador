# Headline and lead patterns

Use these for a landing page, product page, or offer page headline. Write the promise and mechanism
first ([sales argument](sales-argument.md)), then draft 5–10 headlines from different patterns and
keep the headline that passes the tests below. Examples use invented products.

| Pattern | Example | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| **Outcome in a time frame** | Build next week's clinic rota in 40 minutes | Proof supports the time |
| **Outcome without the usual cost** | Fill every shift without the Sunday-night group chat | The pain of the old way is vivid |
| **Named before and after** | Northpier went from 6 hours of rota work to 40 minutes | One strong result; its source sits right under the headline |
| **Turn input into outcome** | Turn last month's support tickets into next month's FAQ | The input is something they already have |
| **Category for audience** | Shift scheduling for dental clinics | The visitor must first know what it is |
| **The one different thing** | The rota tool that asks staff before it assigns | Crowded market; the mechanism is the news |
| **Never again** | Never rebuild a rota because one person called in sick | The mechanism truly prevents it |
| **No skill needed** | Payroll-ready timesheets, no spreadsheet formulas | The reader doubts they can do it |
| **Objection turned around** | Why a $49 tool costs less than your free spreadsheet | You can show the arithmetic |
| **Tried that** | If shift apps did not stop the swaps, the rules were the problem | Solution-aware readers burned before |
| **Offer first** | Your first rota built with you, free for 14 days | Warm traffic that knows the product |
| **News** | New: rotas that respect each nurse's preferences | Product-aware readers and launches |
| **Question in their words** | Still building the rota on Sunday night? | Only when every target reader answers yes |

## Test each headline

- It speaks to a want or pain the reader already has, in words from their reviews, calls, or tickets.
- A busy reader understands it at a glance. Plain words beat clever ones.
- It promises a reward for reading on, and the next lines pay it off.
- It has a concrete detail: a number, a name, a time, a picture. If a competitor could run it
  unchanged, add the detail that only you can claim.
- It promises no more than the proof supports.

The headline carries the promise. The subhead carries who it is for, the mechanism, and the best
proof point. Read headline and subhead together, then read the first screen of the page alone.

## Leads

The lead is the first lines after the headline. It turns attention into reading. Choose by awareness:

- **Offer lead** (knows and trusts you): the deal, terms, and action in the first lines.
- **Promise lead** (knows the product category): the outcome and why it is believable.
- **Problem lead** (knows the problem): their situation in their words, then the hidden cause.
- **Mechanism lead** (has tried alternatives): the different way and why it works.
- **Fact lead** (skeptical, crowded market): one verified, surprising fact with its source.
- **Story lead** (does not know the problem yet): a short scene with someone like them.

Cut the warm-up. If the first paragraph can be deleted without loss, delete it.
