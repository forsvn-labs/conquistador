# Brand character from the brief

Method version: `brand-character-v1`. Define how this product should behave and communicate in
its actual situations. This is a design working method, not a taxonomy of universal personalities,
a psychological assessment, or a prediction of audience emotions. Prior archetype labels and blend
percentages are historical context only; do not convert them into validated character decisions.

## Identify the evidence and the choices

Record the product task, the audience evidence supplied, current behavior, operator preferences,
and hard constraints. Mark each statement observed, supplied but unverified, or proposed. A founder's
preference may be a valid creative choice; it must not masquerade as research about the audience.
A category or demographic does not establish fear, aspiration, taste, or emotional need.

Find situations where character affects an actual decision: explaining a limitation, teaching a
new task, asking for payment, responding to an error, or celebrating a completed action. Select the
situations the product has. Do not invent an origin struggle, comeback, user pain, or sharing ritual.

## Draft a character statement

Describe a chosen manner of working and its practical boundary. For example, a synthetic scheduling
product might choose: “Keep coordination visible and easy to correct.” This choice could support a
clear owner label and undo action. It does not require a particular color, font, or corner radius.

For each character priority, record:

| Field | What to decide |
|---|---|
| Basis | Brief fact, observed behavior, or explicit creative preference |
| Situation | Where the priority changes a product or communication decision |
| Intended behavior | What the brand does in that situation |
| Boundary | What it avoids, with a concrete counterexample |
| Evidence needed | What would confirm the choice or show it is unsuitable |

Use as many priorities as the brief needs. If two compete, identify the situation in which each
wins. Concise routine instructions and detailed failure explanations can coexist. They do not need
a numerical blend or a rule that assigns one personality to the entire product.

## Compare visual options without pretending they are inevitable

Translate a behavior into options, not a symbol dictionary. Choose a representative surface and
hold its content constant while comparing the relevant difference. Explain both why an option
might help and what it could cost.

- **Density:** compare how much task information fits and how easily related items can be grouped.
  Check the actual labels and screen sizes; “calm” does not automatically mean more whitespace.
- **Shape:** compare corners on the actual controls, cards, and nested surfaces. Consider clipping,
  padding, alignment, focus outlines, and consistency. Rounded does not prove safety; sharp does
  not prove authority. Token values are implementation choices to inspect, not personality scores.
- **Type:** compare legibility, character coverage, hierarchy, small text, numbers, and licensing.
  Treat perceived tone as a hypothesis or preference. A font class does not establish trust.
- **Color:** assign roles and compare contrast, state distinction, nearby assets, and selected themes.
  Record any desired association as an untested interpretation. Do not infer a universal emotional
  effect or waive accessibility because a color seems to suit the brand.
- **Imagery:** choose what the image explains and what evidence it represents. Distinguish real,
  permitted product/customer imagery from a clearly representative illustration. Avoid copying
  another brand's recognizable expression as a shortcut to the desired character.
- **Motion:** name the state change it clarifies, the non-motion alternative, and interruption rules.
  A playful brief does not justify obscuring an error, blocking an action, or ignoring reduced motion.

Make one recommendation with its basis and rejected alternative. A short list of unresolved options
is better than an invented audience finding. Do not require choices that the requested quick route
does not need. An operator can choose a preference without a user study; report it as a preference.

## Test the character at a difficult moment

Use a real or explicitly synthetic friction case. Does the product explain what happened, what is
known, and what the person can do next? Does the visual or verbal choice obscure a condition or
punish refusal? If it does, change the choice even if it matches an earlier mood description.

An intended feeling is a hypothesis. Record the design behavior and how it might support that
feeling without saying it causes it. A rendered sample can demonstrate appearance; it cannot prove
trust, belonging, retention, or conversion. Mark actual user feedback separately from agent review.

## Handoff contract and migration

Write `character_method: brand-character-v1` and a **Brand Character** section with statement,
priorities, boundaries, and basis. Keep **Personality Traits**, **Emotional Journey Map**,
**CORE TENSION**, and **DESIGN PROMISE** headings for human-document continuity, with these meanings:
traits are chosen behaviors; emotions are intended and unverified unless evidence exists; tension
is a supplied task gap or N/A; the promise is an operational commitment within current capability.
Do not carry old 70/30 fields forward. Consumers reading an older **Brand Archetype** section must
request or produce a fresh, explicitly provisional character interpretation, never infer tokens
from its label. Preserve older approved documents rather than rewriting their historical meaning.

Voice and visual agents consume the chosen behaviors and brief evidence. Token agents implement
approved choices; critics check traceability, readability, consistency, and accessibility evidence.
No critic should fail a design merely because its shapes contradict a personality stereotype.
